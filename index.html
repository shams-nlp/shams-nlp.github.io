TL;DR

Our novel contribution is an evaluation that ties diacritization, G2P, ASR, and A2P together over the same utterances, grounded in audio and stratified by LA variety.

Abstract

Levantine Arabic (LA) is spoken by tens of millions of people, creating a pressing need for shared benchmarks to evaluate LA speech-language technologies. Evaluating such technology is particularly challenging given LA’s internal diversity and its opaque and non-standardized orthography.

We present SHAMS (SHami Annotated Multi-dialect Speech), a benchmark comprising 1,300 utterances drawn from open audio corpora, balanced across five LA varieties (Urban and Rural Palestinian, and Urban Jordanian, Lebanese, and Syrian). Each utterance is represented across four aligned tiers: audio, unvocalized orthography, diacritized text, and phonetic transcription. This structure supports evaluation of various downstream tasks such as diacritization, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, automatic speech recognition, and audio-to-phoneme, grounded in audio and stratified by variety. We benchmark open and proprietary models across these tasks to demonstrate the utility of this benchmark for measuring progress across LA.

Contributions

  • A balanced spoken corpus of 1,300 utterances drawn from open audio sources, covering five Levantine varieties
  • Multi-tier annotations aligned to each utterance consisting of diacritized Arabic and phonetic transcription, thus enabling joint evaluation of diacritization, G2P, ASR, and A2P.
  • Baseline results across open and proprietary models, stratified by variety, demonstrating the benchmark’s utility for distinguishing model performance across tasks and varieties.

Four aligned tiers

Each utterance in SHAMS includes four aligned tiers:

  1. (i)the audio segment;
  2. (ii)unvocalized Arabic orthography, the consonantal text as conventionally written, corrected when needed to match the recording; بقدر أروح بكرا
  3. (iii)fully diacritized Arabic, the same text with conventional diacritics matching the pronunciation in the audio; بَقدَر أَرُوح بُكرَا
  4. (iv)a phonetic transcription in a Semitic transcription system. bakdar ʾarūḥ bučra

Four tasks

  • (ii) → (iii) · DERDiacritizationRestoring vowel and gemination marks.
  • (ii) → (iv) · CERG2PGrapheme-to-phoneme conversion: mapping orthography to phonetic transcription.
  • (i) → (ii) · WER, CERASRAutomatic speech recognition: transcribing audio to text.
  • (i) → (iv) · CERA2PAudio-to-phoneme conversion: transcribing audio to phonetic transcription.

Why a phonetic tier

ق

Even fully diacritized orthography leaves dialectal pronunciation underspecified: for example, the realization of ق is not recoverable from the script. Automating this transcription could relieve the bottleneck, but for LA there has been no way to measure how well current systems perform the task.

Across Palestinian varieties, ق (/q/) surfaces as:

  • Urban speech[ʔ]a glottal stop · ba’dir
  • Rural speech[k]a voiceless velar · bakdir
  • Bedouin speech[ɡ]a voiced velar · bagdir

Composition

SHAMS comprises 1,300 utterances totaling roughly 15,500 words and just over two hours of speech, spread across five Levantine varieties. The benchmark is built on the Levantine portions of two open multi-dialectal Arabic speech corpora, Casablanca and LangMedia.

Annotation reliability

93.2%agreement over 7,290 diacritic slots
κ = 0.898Cohen’s kappa, 95% CI [0.886, 0.909]

A second native speaker of Palestinian Arabic independently re-annotated a stratified subset of 150 utterances, 30 per variety, under the same audio-guided protocol. Agreement is high (κ ≥ 0.86) across all varieties.

For 520 initial utterances, phonetic transcriptions were produced by hand and aligned to the Arabic orthography to place diacritics. For the remaining 780 utterances, Levanti produced an automatic first pass over both tiers, which annotators then corrected. Annotators worked against the audio throughout, and every utterance was reviewed by an author.
